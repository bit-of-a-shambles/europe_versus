Europe's innovation debate often begins with a funeral for European ingenuity. The patent data does not support it. In 2025, the EU produced **153 European patent applications per million residents**, compared with 135 from the United States. Finland, Sweden, Denmark and the Netherlands all sat far above either aggregate.

Switzerland led the ranking with 1,096 applications per million people. Finland recorded 613; Sweden and Denmark about 446 each. Germany, Europe's largest economy, filed 293 per million. The United States remained the largest origin in absolute numbers, but on this per-person measure it ranked below a cluster of smaller European countries.

There is an important home-field advantage. These are applications at the European Patent Office, so a European company is more likely than an American one to use this route. Patents also vary enormously in quality and commercial value. One filing is not one invention, and an application is not yet a granted patent. The chart is evidence of inventive activity, not proof that Europe has a better innovation economy.

That distinction points to the real weakness. The IMF estimates that young US firms have a combined market value of $42.9 trillion, against $5 trillion for young EU firms. Its work also finds that a mature European company averages about 10 employees, compared with 70 in the United States. Europe creates knowledge, then too often fails to give the resulting company a continental market, deep risk capital or a simple path to expansion.

The policy failure therefore sits after the laboratory as much as inside it. Twenty-seven national company-law regimes, uneven insolvency rules, fragmented capital markets and country-by-country product barriers turn expansion into repeated negotiation. Public research budgets matter, but they cannot compensate for a market that makes scale unusually expensive.

The patent tally shifts the burden of proof. A lack of ideas cannot explain the whole gap. Europe now has to explain why so few of its inventions become firms large enough to lift productivity and wages.

For a decade, the simplest American complaint about European defence was also the strongest: Europe was not paying enough. That argument has lost much of its force. NATO's European members and Canada raised defence spending from **1.4% of their combined GDP in 2014 to 2.3% in 2025**. Every Ally met or exceeded the old 2% guideline last year.

The acceleration came late. The aggregate barely moved before 2019, rose during the pandemic, then climbed from 1.63% in 2022 to an estimated 2.27% three years later. At constant 2021 prices, NATO says Europe and Canada spent more than $571 billion in 2025, over $90 billion more than in 2024.

Money is an input. It does not tell us how quickly an army can replace ammunition, whether national systems can communicate, or how many incompatible versions of the same weapon Europe buys. A continent can meet a spending ratio and still get poor military value from the last euro.

The aggregate also needs care. It includes Canada, and its membership changes as Montenegro, North Macedonia, Finland and Sweden join during the period. The latest two years are estimates. The line nevertheless captures a shift too large to dismiss as accounting: defence budgets moved sharply upward across the alliance's European side.

The next argument should be about production. Short national orders encourage small runs and high unit costs. Separate procurement rules duplicate development and make maintenance harder. Dependence on American systems can be sensible for interoperability, but it becomes a vulnerability when Europe cannot replenish basic stocks without waiting on factories elsewhere.

There is a pro-growth case for doing this well. Predictable joint orders can support new plants, specialised suppliers and research that spills into civilian technology. Procurement that protects national incumbents regardless of performance will produce the opposite: more spending with little added capacity.

Europe has finally answered the old bill-sharing criticism. It should not confuse that with having solved defence. From here, judge the new money by delivery times, replenishment rates and equipment that allies can actually use together.
